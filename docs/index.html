Attackers are currently exploiting a critical vulnerability tracked as CVE-2026-76466 in Cisco Expressway that allows them to remotely execute commands as root without any authentication, according to warnings from the US government and security researchers. CVE-2026-76466 , which carries a 9. 8 CVSS rating, affects Cisco Expressway-C and Cisco Expressway-E gateways configured in clusters, both virtual and physical appliances running vulnerable software.

These provide firewall traversal, VPN-less remote access, and call routing for remote users connecting to voice, video, and messaging collaboration services. The bug involves hard-coded authentication credentials and insufficient certificate validation in clustered Expressway deployments. “An attacker with possession of the cookies could exploit this vulnerability by connecting to the database service and invoking specific remote procedure call (RPC) commands,” Cisco said in October, when it disclosed the bug and issued a software update that fixed the flaw.

“A successful exploit could allow the attacker to execute arbitrary commands as root on the underlying operating system of an affected device. ” At the time, the networking vendor said it wasn’t aware of any abuse of the vulnerability. That all changed late last week, however, when Russian security firm F6 said it was investigating “the first observed case” of miscreants exploiting CVE-2026-76466, shortly after a public proof-of-concept exploit became available.

One Australian manufacturer made the interesting choice of building Bluetooth into a trailer brake controller without adequately securing its critical braking functions. Anyone within Bluetooth range of an unpatched Elecbrakes trailer controller could potentially trigger its brakes or apply more braking force than intended, without needing to authenticate.

The discovery comes courtesy of a pair of security researchers from Element Digital, Patrick Forck and Jeremy McCarthy. Their investigation began earlier this year when Forck was driving down the road and noticed that his physical Elecbrakes In-Car Remote had connected to someone else's trailer without pairing or prompting him. “It had connected mid-drive, without pairing or a prompt, to a brake controller that wasn’t mine, on a trailer I’d never seen, belonging to a driver who had no idea,” the researchers wrote .

That one-off connection triggered a full-scale investigation by the pair. For those unfamiliar with trailer brake controllers, these devices are commonly used on larger trailers. Australian manufacturer Elecbrakes sells such controllers in Australia and New Zealand.
