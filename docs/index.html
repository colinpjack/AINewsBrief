This follows an investigation the regulator started back in May into Microsoft’s software portfolio including Word, Excel, Outlook, PowerPoint and Teams, plus its security, server and operating systems. The watchdog is also probing whether Microsoft enjoys a position of “substantial and entrenched market power” through the suite of software that could be seen as stifling competition and harming buyer choice. It lays out its proposed decision in a document [PDF] and is inviting comments that must be submitted by November 13.

The CMA will make its final decision by February 13, 2027. Bargain spotter The CMA makes it clear the company holds a strong position in each of the five areas it investigated (personal computer operating systems, productivity software suites, security software, server operating systems and relational database management systems), and that customers face barriers when switching providers – limiting their choice and weakening the ability of other software developers to offer rival products. Its proposal document notes Microsoft has been by far the largest provider of business software in the UK for decades.

When a million veterans shared their genetic data by enrolling in the Department of Veterans Affairs' “Million Veteran Program,” they expected nothing back. This did not surprise Ravi Madduri, a senior scientist with the Data Science and Learning Division at the U. Department of Energy’s (DOE) Argonne National Laboratory.

He is abundantly clear about the terms under which these veterans donated their data: “Even after giving everything to the country, they found it in their hearts to give more without expecting anything in return. ” That gift, which constituted the nation’s largest biorepository and most ancestrally varied cohorts of veteran data in the world, created a debt that Madduri, who also holds a joint appointment in the Department of Human Genetics at the University of Chicago, is working to repay. “What I feel when I look at that data is that I have an obligation to deliver to the veterans on their intentions in doing this,” he says.

Those intentions, as he reads it, are that others, veteran or not, can benefit from whatever the data can reveal. “We cannot dilly dally,” Madduri says. After arriving at Argonne as a graduate student in 2001, Madduri has spent the last two decades at the intersection of supercomputing and biomedicine, much of the time building the computing that turns the veterans’ data into results impacting areas from post-traumatic stress disorder to heart disease to cancer.
