Simson Garfinkel's article is simply called EMACS turns 50, but the sub-heading explains why it matters more: "How the MIT AI Lab's text editor gave birth to the free software movement.

" There are multiple timelines and accounts of the origins of Emacs, and it is not a single monolithic application. The name is an abbreviation for " E diting MAC ro S ", and the original editor for which this set of macros was developed was TECO – "Text Editor and COrrector," written by Dan Murphy in 1962. Murphy died earlier this year at the age of 83.

The inimitable Jamie Zawinski has an encyclopedic Emacs Timeline – as he mentions at the start, his interest being that he long ago maintained a fork of GNU Emacs, originally called Lucid Emacs . It's still around, under its later name XEmacs. There are a lot of forks and re-implementations of Emacs out there, and we are not going to go into all of them, or this article would become a book – one only interesting to programmer-archeologists (to use a term coined by the late great Vernor Vinge ).
