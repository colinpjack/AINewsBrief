As the world’s billionaires anxiously plot datacenters to run trillions of lines of AI-generated slop code, one software engineer has zagged in a completely different direction: building an ecosystem for writing and running small apps that run on desktop computers, like it was 1992 all over again. Self-described “maker of things” Tarwin Stroh-Spijer has created a JavaScript runtime, called TinyJS, for making very small applications (many occupying ~6MB or less on disk) for macOS, Linux or Windows. To be clear, TinyJS is not a “no code” framework with hobbled capabilities.

It offers the full programming power of JavaScript, along with the visual rendering and communication capabilities of the underlying OS. The trick to TinyJS’s frugality is that it uses the WebView window of each OS, utilizing the built-in browser engine to visually render the app, rather than implementing a UI layer from scratch. It uses WebKit to run apps in this fashion on macOS, WebView2 for Windows, and WebKitGTK for Linux.

TinyJS’s approach is starkly different from using the Electron framework, which bundles a Chromium open source browser core with each desktop app. But whereas Electron promises an identical look-and-feel of an app across all platforms, TinyJS apps will have slight visual variances when run on the different platforms, owing to minor differences in how each OS’s rendering engine works. The Electron app pays for this cross-platform uniformity: with Electron, even the most basic application can weigh 150MB.

Researchers have demonstrated a way to control where 2D semiconductor crystals begin growing, potentially addressing an obstacle to manufacturing next-generation chips at scale, according to a study published in Nature. The paper, "Spatially deterministic nucleation of 2D semiconductors by etching flux," was authored by a team including researchers at KAIST and South Korean startup TDS Innovation and published on October 7. The technique uses an etching flux to suppress the formation of new crystals everywhere within each patterned growth region except at its geometric center.

Two-dimensional materials are atomically thin rather than literally two-dimensional. Graphene, perhaps the best-known example, is a single layer of carbon atoms, but its lack of an intrinsic band gap makes it unsuitable for conventional switching transistors. Monolayers of some transition-metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), such as molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂), do have a band gap and are being investigated as materials for future transistors and other nanoscale devices.

When grown on a wafer, however, 2D semiconductor crystals typically start forming at random points, or "nucleation events occur stochastically at random sites within the growth region," as the paper puts it. This means that multiple crystals form, and as they grow and meet, they create boundaries between them that degrade electrical performance and make devices less uniform. To encourage a single crystal to form in each patterned region, the researchers developed what they call an etching-flux-mediated single-centred nucleation (EF-SCN) process.
