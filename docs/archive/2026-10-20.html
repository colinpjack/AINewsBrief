For years, cyber security was treated as a specialist operational discipline that was essential but largely delegated to technical teams. That model is no longer sustainable. Pressure is now coming from three directions at once, as policy raises expectations for boards and senior leaders, organizational reform reshapes how services, systems and suppliers connect, and technology change, from cloud and hybrid work to AI, expands both opportunity and exposure.

Together, these forces mean cyber resilience can no longer sit below board level. Public services, from healthcare and benefits to transport, education, local government and emergency response, are now so digitally dependent that a cyber incident is not simply an IT outage. It is a risk to public trust, public safety and the continuity of government itself.

Europe’s drive for cloud sovereignty is leading to member states favoring providers from their own nation instead of building a Europe-wide cloud services market, according to a report critical of the EU’s proposed Cloud and AI Development Act ( CADA ). A study by the European Centre for International Political Economy (ECIPE) looked at public cloud contracts in nine EU member states and found that every one that invoked the new vocabulary around “sovereignty” went to a domestic provider, rather than one based in another EU country. It says that European cloud procurement was already highly concentrated nationally before sovereignty became a key policy objective, based on analysis of 8,725 cloud-related procurement contracts between 2018 and 2025.

Nearly all went to providers in the same country with negligible intra-EU cross-border competition. Furthermore, the report claims that those domestic wins often turn out to be from prime contractors that are relying on infrastructure provided by the big hyperscale operators, where sovereignty attaches to the nationality of the operator, not to the infrastructure layer on which the service depends. At issue is a conceptual confusion over cloud sovereignty, which is being used to represent two different problems, according to ECIPE.

One treats sovereignty as a security and resilience objective, securing data and managing risks. Sovereignty here means that the user has choices over the provider and design of their cloud service needs. The second treats sovereignty as an industrial policy objective, where sovereignty is more about the notion of Europe-headquartered firms becoming more successful if they are not exposed to competition with non-European providers.

A 16-year-old security flaw in Broadcom's Wi-Fi software could let attackers hijack millions of routers without knowing the network password, even though the chipmaker reportedly knew about the problem since 2018. Security researcher Quentin Kaiser disclosed the vulnerability , dubbed "WPSplinter," revealing two bugs in Broadcom's proprietary Wi-Fi implementation that date back to at least 2010. An attempted fix arrived eight years later, but Kaiser claims it didn't fully resolve the problem.

The bugs lie in Broadcom's handling of Wi-Fi Protected Setup (WPS), the feature that lets users connect devices to a wireless network without manually entering a password. Kaiser found that specially crafted WPS messages could corrupt the router's memory, allowing someone within Wi-Fi range to run their own code on the device. They wouldn't need the Wi-Fi password, and the router's owner wouldn't have to do a thing.

Kaiser demonstrated working exploits against routers supplied by Belgian internet providers Proximus and Orange. In one attack against an ASKEY TCG3000 gateway, he ran code on the device, pulled its Wi-Fi password from memory, and sent it back over the air. He then used the stolen credentials to join the network.
